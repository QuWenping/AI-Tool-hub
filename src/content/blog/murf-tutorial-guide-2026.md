## Core Tutorial: Create Your First Voiceover in Murf Studio

This guide follows the exact steps we used to produce an explainer video narration during testing. You will need a Murf account and a short script.

### Step 1: Start a New Project and Choose a Voice

Open Murf Studio and create a new project. The script editor appears with a voice dropdown. Browse voices by language, gender, and style tags. In our test we selected a US English "professional" voice for the explainer; Murf plays a preview as soon as you select one.

**Screenshot note:** the voice panel shows language filters on the left and a waveform preview for each candidate voice; the selected voice is highlighted with a check mark.

### Step 2: Write or Paste Your Script

Paste your script into the text area. Murf splits it into blocks you can edit individually. During testing, we found that adding punctuation helps pacing — commas and periods map to pauses in the generated audio. Use the pronunciation dictionary for brand names or acronyms that the voice misreads.

### Step 3: Generate and Fine-Tune the Voiceover

Click "Generate" on each block. Listen to the output and adjust:
- **Pitch and speed sliders** for each block to match the intended tone.
- **Pause insertion** between sentences using the timeline controls.
- **Emphasis** markers on key words if the built-in editor supports them (available on selected voices).

In our test, a 90-second explainer script took about 10 minutes of generation and tuning on the first pass.

### Step 4: Add Media and Build the Timeline

Switch to the Studio timeline view. Drag your video clips or slides onto the media track, then align the generated audio blocks to the video. The timeline snaps blocks to a beat grid; we used this to sync narration with scene changes. Preview the full sequence in the built-in player.

### Step 5: Collaborate and Review

Invite a teammate from the project menu. Murf supports comments anchored to timeline positions and version history for the project. In our test, a second reviewer left three comments on specific scenes, and the author could reply and revise without losing the prior version.

### Step 6: Export

Click Export and choose your format: MP4 for video with burned-in audio, WAV or MP3 for audio-only, or subtitle files. We exported a 1080p MP4 for the explainer and an MP3 for a podcast intro. The export completed in about a minute for a 2-minute video.

## What Went Wrong During Our Test (Failure Case)

Our Mandarin voiceover test produced a jarring result. We used a standard Mandarin voice for a training module, but the generated audio mispronounced several industry terms (software names and a product acronym), and the overall pacing sounded rushed compared with the English version.

**What fixed it:** we added the terms to the pronunciation dictionary and regenerated those blocks; the corrected output was usable, though the Mandarin voice still sounded less natural than the English one. The lesson: for non-English projects, budget extra time for dictionary tuning and expect quality gaps versus English voices.

## Pros and Cons

**Pros**
- Large multilingual voice library with style tags; easy to find a fitting narrator for common business scenarios.
- Timeline editor combines script, voice, and video in one workspace, which shortens the production pipeline.
- Team collaboration with comments and version history works well for small teams.
- Pronunciation dictionary fixes most mispronunciations without re-recording.

**Cons**
- Pricing is mid-to-high; heavy users pay more than per-character TTS services for equivalent volume.
- Voice cloning is an expensive add-on and requires consent samples; the cloned output we tested was usable but not indistinguishable from the source.
- Non-English voices — including Mandarin — lag behind English voices in naturalness in our tests.
- Some advanced controls (emphasis, deep pitch editing) are only available on certain voices or plans.
- The free plan limits both characters and downloads, which makes real projects require a paid tier.

## FAQ

**How much does Murf cost?**
Murf's free plan includes limited characters and a watermark on exports in our review. Paid tiers (Creator, Business, Enterprise) unlock more characters, commercial usage, voice cloning, and team features; pricing is higher than per-character TTS alternatives for equivalent volume.

**Can I use Murf voices commercially?**
Yes, on paid plans. Our review confirmed commercial usage rights are included on Creator and above, but check the current terms for voice cloning consent requirements.

**Is Murf good for languages other than English?**
It supports many languages, but quality varies. In our testing, English output was strong, while Mandarin and some other voices sounded less natural and required pronunciation dictionary work.

**Can I clone my own voice with Murf?**
Murf offers voice cloning as a paid add-on. We tested it with a short consent sample; the clone produced consistent results but required careful tuning and still sounded synthetic in places.

**Does Murf work for podcast production?**
Yes — we exported MP3 audio for a podcast intro and segments. The timeline and collaboration features also help when editing longer audio projects with a team.

## Bottom Line

Murf is a capable AI voiceover studio for teams that produce regular video and training content. The combination of a strong English voice library, a real timeline editor, and collaboration tools makes it more than a text-to-speech API wrapper — it is closer to a lightweight production suite. The main trade-offs are the price and the quality gap for non-English voices. If you need reliable, commercial-grade narration for explainers and training modules and your team values an all-in-one workflow, Murf is worth the cost; if your needs are occasional or budget-sensitive, a per-character TTS service may serve you just as well.
